Two-Factor ANOVA — main effects and the interaction

Lecture 7 · Course 2.1 Methods & Statistics · Bruno Bocanegra · Erasmus University Rotterdam

A note before you start

Sezen — this lecture only adds one idea to Theme 6, and it is worth naming it before you start so the arithmetic does not disguise it. Everything about the machine stays the same. The one new thing is that the between-groups variation now splits into three named pieces instead of staying whole, and the third piece — the interaction — is the reason anyone runs this design.

You already think in interactions at work, every day. “Does the new review format improve ratings?” is a main effect. “It improves ratings for first-time managers and does nothing for experienced ones” is an interaction, and it is almost always the finding that actually changes what you do. A DEI intervention with no main effect that works strongly for one group and not another is the same shape as this lecture’s example — no main effect of fandom, and yet fandom decides everything. That is why “examine the interaction first” is a rule and not a preference.

Work through the lessons in order; they follow the slides. The diagram in each one carries the argument, so look at it before you read the bullets. The numbers are the lecture’s own throughout — 25, 80, 1540, 320, 720, 500 — and if you can say where each of those six numbers comes from, you can rebuild the whole table from scratch.

🎯 Learning objectives

  • Recognise a two-factor design from a description, and name its factors, levels and cells.
  • State the three null hypotheses a two-factor ANOVA tests.
  • Explain what a main effect is, and why “main” does not mean “most important”.
  • Define an interaction in two equivalent ways: an unanticipated combination, and an effect that differs across the levels of another factor.
  • Split SS Between into SS(A), SS(B) and SS(A × B), and compute each one from a table of means.
  • Split the degrees of freedom the same way, including df(A × B) = df(A) × df(B).
  • Complete a two-factor ANOVA table and compute all three F values against a shared MS Within.
  • Compare each F with its critical value and state a p range, a decision and a conclusion in words.
  • Read an interaction plot: parallel means no interaction, non-parallel means interaction.
  • Say which conclusions a design licenses — causal for a manipulated factor, associational for an observed one.
  • List the advantages of a two-factor design, including the one no series of one-way ANOVAs can deliver.
  • Describe the Dove, Byrne &amp; Bruce (2009) findings and explain why the interaction is the whole story.

🏛️ The argument in one paragraph

A two-factor ANOVA takes the machine you already know and splits its between-groups part into three. Two factors sort people into cells; the spread of those cell means around the grand mean is SS Between, and it divides into SS(A), SS(B) and whatever is left over, which is the interaction. The degrees of freedom divide the same way, with the interaction df a product rather than a sum. Each piece becomes a mean square and is divided by one shared MS Within, giving three F values tested against the same table. The two main effects ask whether each factor matters on its own, averaging the other away; the interaction asks whether the effect of one depends on the level of the other, which is the one question a stack of separate one-way ANOVAs can never answer. And it is the question that usually matters most: in the lecture’s own example fandom has no main effect at all, and yet it decides whether the music adds two points or twenty-two — exactly as, in Dove, Byrne and Bruce (2009), a thinking style with no main effect on depression decides whether depression tracks BMI at all. Which is why the rule is: plot the cell means, read the interaction first, and only then tell a story about the main effects.

🧭 From one factor to two — what actually changes

One factor you know, two factors you are about to

The lecture opens by walking back through the one-factor ANOVA you already know: one grouping variable, several groups, one F test. Everything in today’s lecture is built on that machine. You are not learning a new test — you are learning what happens when a second grouping variable is added to the same study.

  • 🏷️A FACTOR is a categorical variable that sorts people into groups. Its categories are its LEVELS.
  • 1️⃣One factor with k levels → one-factor ANOVA, one F test, the question “do these k means differ?”
  • 2️⃣TWO factors → a two-factor (two-way) ANOVA, which answers three questions from one set of data.
  • 📏The dependent variable is unchanged: one quantitative score, one score per person.
  • ⚙️The engine is unchanged too. F is still a signal-to-noise ratio and MS Within is still the noise.
🪝 Memory hook
Same machine, one extra conveyor belt. The between-groups part splits into three instead of staying whole.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“Last week I compared three kinds of music on one score. This week there are two things sorting people at once — the music AND whether they’re a fan — and one analysis tells me about each of them separately plus whether they depend on each other.”
★ Key insight
Adding a second factor does not add a second analysis. It splits the between-groups variation you already had into three named pieces: factor A, factor B, and the interaction between them.
🧪 Demo 1 — one factor, two factors, or neither?
Pick one. You get the reasoning either way.

🧩 Comprehension check
A study measures exam score across four teaching methods. One factor or two?
One. “Teaching method” is a single categorical variable with four levels — k = 4. That is a one-factor ANOVA, exactly like Theme 6.
Now the same study also records whether each student is a first-year or a second-year, and compares all of those groups. One factor or two?

Lecture 2 of 16 · the design · slides 14–19

🏗️ The design — factors, levels, cells

Factor × factor = cells, and the name of the design

Before anything is computed, you have to be able to read a design off the page. Three words do all the work: factor, level, cell. Get them right and every df in the whole analysis falls out of them automatically.

  • 🏷️FACTOR — one of the two categorical variables. This lecture calls them A and B; the study calls them Sound and Fan.
  • 🪜LEVEL — one category of one factor. Sound has two levels; Fan has two levels.
  • 🔲CELL — one combination of one level of A with one level of B. 2 × 2 = 4 cells.
  • ✖️The design is named by its levels: this is a 2 × 2. A factor with 3 levels crossed with one with 2 would be a 2 × 3, with 6 cells.
  • 🧍BOTH factors are between-subjects here: every person sits in exactly one cell and gives exactly ONE score.
number of cells = levels of A × levels of B N = cells × n per cell
🪝 Memory hook
Levels times levels equals cells. The name of the design IS the multiplication.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“The design is a grid. One factor runs across the top, the other down the side, and every box in the grid is a different group of people.”
★ Key insight
Every person contributes one score to exactly one cell. That single sentence is what makes this a between-subjects design — and it is what the whole degrees-of-freedom bookkeeping depends on.
🎬 Watch: The design🎬 Watch: a two-factor worked example
🧩 Comprehension check
A study crosses three doses of caffeine with two times of day. Name the design and count the cells.
A 3 × 2 (or 2 × 3) two-factor design, with 6 cells. If each cell holds 8 people, N = 48.
Thirty people each do the task once with music and once in silence. Is that this kind of design?
No. Each person gives two scores, so the groups are not independent — that is a within-subject (repeated-measures) design. Today’s ANOVA assumes one score per person.
Lecture 3 of 16 · the logic · slides 14–19

❓ Three questions, one analysis

Main effect of A, main effect of B, and the interaction

This is the heart of the lecture. A two-factor ANOVA asks three questions, and it answers all three from the same table of data. Each question gets its own sum of squares, its own degrees of freedom, its own F and its own verdict.

  • ⬇️MAIN EFFECT OF A — does factor A change the score, averaging over all the levels of B?
  • ➡️MAIN EFFECT OF B — does factor B change the score, averaging over all the levels of A?
  • 🔀INTERACTION A × B — does the effect of A DEPEND on which level of B you are looking at?
  • 📏A main effect is a comparison of MARGINAL means: the row means, or the column means.
  • 🧩The interaction is about the PATTERN of the cell means, which no single pair of marginal means can show you.
🪝 Memory hook
Columns, rows, pattern. Three questions, three F values, one table.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“The analysis asks three things at once: does the first thing matter on its own, does the second thing matter on its own, and do the two of them work differently in combination than you’d guess from each one alone.”
★ Key insight
The word “main” in main effect does not mean “most important”. It means “that factor on its own, with the other one averaged away”. A factor with no main effect can still be the most interesting thing in the study — which is exactly what happens in the lecture’s own example.
🎬 Watch: Three questions, one analysis🎬 Watch: a two-factor worked example
🧩 Comprehension check
In a 2 × 2 of Sound × Fan, which means does the main effect of Sound compare?
The two COLUMN means — the average for everyone who heard the weather report against the average for everyone who heard Michael Jackson, fans and non-fans pooled together.
What would “no interaction” look like in words?
The effect of sound is the same size for fans as for non-fans. Whatever the music does, it does equally to both groups.

🎧 The study — the “Michael Jackson Effect”

Twenty people, four cells, one spatial awareness score

The lecture carries one example through every step, so it is worth fixing it in your head now. Twenty people listen either to Michael Jackson or to a weather report, and each one is also already either a fan of Michael Jackson or not. Everyone then takes a spatial awareness test.

  • 📐Dependent variable: spatial awareness score — quantitative, one number per person.
  • 🎧Factor A: SOUND, with two levels — Michael Jackson, or a weather report.
  • 💚Factor B: FAN, with two levels — a fan of Michael Jackson, or not.
  • 🔢Four cells of five people each, so N = 20.
  • ❓The question the lecture is really chasing: does the music help, and does it help everyone equally?
🪝 Memory hook
Twenty people, four cells, five each. Grand mean 25.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“Half of them hear Michael Jackson and half hear a weather report, and separately, some of them are fans and some aren’t. Then everybody does a spatial test, and we see what moved the scores.”
★ Key insight
Carrying one example through the whole lecture is deliberate: once you know where 25, 80 and 1540 come from, every later formula is just those numbers in a new arrangement.

This example is teaching data invented for the lecture, not a published experiment. The real published study comes at the end of the lecture, on slides 43–48 — Lesson 16 here.

Lecture 5 of 16 · research design · slides 18–19

🔬 Level of control — an experiment on one factor only

Experimental on one factor, observational on the other

The lecture makes a point of this, and exams love it. In this study one factor was manipulated by the researcher and people were randomly assigned to its levels. The other factor was not manipulated at all — people arrived already sorted. That difference changes what you are allowed to say at the end.

  • 🎛️SOUND was manipulated: the researcher decided who heard what, and assignment was random.
  • 🚫FAN was not manipulated: nobody can be randomly assigned to love Michael Jackson.
  • ⚗️So the study is EXPERIMENTAL with respect to Sound and PASSIVELY OBSERVED with respect to Fan.
  • ✍️For Sound you may say the music CAUSED the change in score. For Fan you may only say the groups DIFFER.
  • ⚠️Fans may differ from non-fans in a dozen other ways — age, musical training, mood — and this design cannot separate them.
🪝 Memory hook
Manipulated and randomised → cause. Brought with them → association.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“We decided what music people heard, so we can blame the music for the difference. We didn’t decide who’s a fan, so for that part all we can say is that fans and non-fans scored differently — not why.”
★ Key insight
A two-factor ANOVA happily mixes an experimental factor and an observed one. The statistics are identical; only the sentence you are allowed to write at the end changes, and it changes per factor.

🧩 Comprehension check
The interaction turns out significant. How should you word it?
Prefer the direction where the manipulated factor is the one doing the acting: “the effect of the sound on spatial awareness depends on whether the listener is a fan”. Saying the music changed who is a fan would be the wrong way round.
Would the arithmetic be any different if BOTH factors had been manipulated?
No — the sums of squares, df and F values are computed the same way. Only the causal language in your conclusion would change.
Lecture 6 of 16 · reading the data · slides 20–21

🔢 Four kinds of mean, and what each one is for

Cell, row, column and grand means — and what each is for

The lecture prints the data as a 2 × 2 table of cell means and variances with the row means, column means and grand mean in the margins. Learning to read that table is half of this theme, because every sum of squares is built out of it.

  • 🔲CELL means — the four raw averages: 20, 22, 18 and 40. The interaction lives here.
  • ➡️ROW means (marginal) — not a fan 21, fan 29. These answer the main effect of FAN.
  • ⬇️COLUMN means (marginal) — weather 19, Michael 31. These answer the main effect of SOUND.
  • 🎯GRAND mean — 25. Every “between” deviation is measured from this one number.
  • 📉The variances in the cells (79, 81, 78, 82) are used only for SS Within — the noise.
row mean = average of the cells in that row column mean = average of the cells in that column
🪝 Memory hook
Cells for the interaction, margins for the main effects, grand mean for everything.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“The four numbers in the middle of the table are the actual group averages. The numbers down the side and along the bottom are those averages collapsed one way or the other, and they’re what the two main effects compare.”
★ Key insight
A marginal mean deliberately throws away information: 29 for the fans mixes a cell mean of 18 with a cell mean of 40. That is exactly why a significant interaction makes marginal means misleading.

Lecture 7 of 16 · hypotheses · slides 23

0️⃣ Three null hypotheses

Two familiar nulls, and one with a different shape

Three questions means three null hypotheses, and in an exam you may be asked to write all three. Two of them look exactly like the ones you already know. The third is different in shape, and it is the one people get wrong.

  • 0️⃣H₀ for A: the population means for the levels of A are equal — the column means do not differ.
  • 0️⃣H₀ for B: the population means for the levels of B are equal — the row means do not differ.
  • 🔀H₀ for A × B: there is NO interaction — the effect of A is the same at every level of B.
  • 🚫Every alternative hypothesis is non-directional: “they are not equal”, “there is an interaction”. There is no one-tailed version.
  • 🎲The three decisions are independent of each other: you may reject none, one, two or all three.
🪝 Memory hook
Two “are the means equal?” and one “does it depend?”.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“Three claims to knock down: the music does nothing, being a fan does nothing, and the two of them don’t depend on each other.”
★ Key insight
The interaction null is not about means being equal. It is about DIFFERENCES being equal — the gap produced by A among the B₁ people equals the gap produced by A among the B₂ people.
🎬 Watch: Three null hypotheses🎬 Watch: a two-factor worked example
🧩 Comprehension check
Write H₀ for the main effect of Sound in words.
In the population, the mean spatial awareness score is the same for people who hear the weather report and people who hear Michael Jackson, averaged over fans and non-fans.
Can the alternative hypothesis for an interaction be one-sided?
No. Like every ANOVA alternative it simply says the null is false — here, that an interaction exists. The F test itself is right-tailed, which is a different thing.
Lecture 8 of 16 · the split · slides 26–30

✂️ Dividing the sums of squares

The between-groups part divides into three

Here is the one structural idea of the whole lecture. The total spread still splits into Between and Within exactly as it did last week — but now the Between part splits again, into the two main effects and the interaction.

  • ➕SS Total = SS Between + SS Within. Unchanged from the one-factor ANOVA.
  • ✂️SS Between now splits three ways: SS(A) + SS(B) + SS(A × B).
  • 🔒SS Within does NOT split. It is still just “people differ from others in their own cell”.
  • 🧮In this study: 1540 = 320 + 720 + 500, and 1540 + 1280 = 2820.
  • ➖The interaction is obtained by SUBTRACTION — it is what the two main effects could not explain. (The three add up like this exactly when the cells hold equal numbers, as here.)
SS(A × B) = SS Between − SS(A) − SS(B)
🪝 Memory hook
Total splits in two; Between splits in three. Within never splits.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“All the variation in the scores gets divided up. Some of it is because of the music, some because of fandom, some because of the particular combination, and the rest is just people being different.”
★ Key insight
The interaction sum of squares is a residual within the between-groups part: it is the variation among the cells that the two factors acting separately cannot account for. That is why you never need a formula for it.
🎬 Watch: Dividing the sums of squares🎬 Watch: a two-factor worked example
🧩 Comprehension check
SS Between = 900, SS(A) = 400, SS(B) = 100. What is SS(A × B)?
900 − 400 − 100 = 400.
Your three between pieces add to more than SS Between. What has gone wrong?
One of the pieces is wrong — most often because a row or column mean was weighted by the wrong n. The three must add to SS Between exactly; that is the check.
Lecture 9 of 16 · calculation · slides 26–32

🧮 Computing each sum of squares

The same sum, four times over

Four sums, and they are the same sum four times: take a mean, subtract the grand mean, square it, multiply by how many people are behind that mean, add them all up. Only the words “cell”, “row” and “column” change.

  • 🔲SS Between = Σ n(cell mean − grand mean)² = 5(20−25)² + 5(22−25)² + 5(18−25)² + 5(40−25)² = 1540.
  • ➡️SS(Fan) = Σ n(row mean − grand mean)² = 10(21−25)² + 10(29−25)² = 320.
  • ⬇️SS(Sound) = Σ n(column mean − grand mean)² = 10(19−25)² + 10(31−25)² = 720.
  • 📉SS Within = Σ (n − 1) × cell variance = 4(79) + 4(81) + 4(78) + 4(82) = 1280.
  • ➖SS(Interaction) = 1540 − 320 − 720 = 500, and SS Total = 1540 + 1280 = 2820.
the n is the number of people BEHIND that mean: 5 for a cell, 10 for a row or a column
🪝 Memory hook
Mean minus grand mean, squared, times its own n. Four times over.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“For each average I work out how far it sits from the overall average, square it so the minus signs don’t cancel, and weight it by how many people that average was built from.”
★ Key insight
SS Within is computed from the cell VARIANCES, not from the means — which is why the two main effects and the interaction cannot touch it. The noise estimate is the same number whatever the groups are doing.

Lecture 10 of 16 · degrees of freedom · slides 31–32

📐 Dividing the degrees of freedom

Everything is minus one — except the interaction

The degrees of freedom split exactly the way the sums of squares do — which is handy, because it gives you a free check. One of these four rules is unlike the others, and it is the one worth memorising.

  • 🔲df Between = number of CELLS − 1 = 4 − 1 = 3.
  • 🪜df(A) = levels of A − 1 = 1. df(B) = levels of B − 1 = 1.
  • ✖️df(A × B) = df(A) × df(B) — a PRODUCT, not a sum. Here 1 × 1 = 1.
  • 🧍df Within = N − number of cells = 20 − 4 = 16.
  • ✅Check: 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 = df Between, and 3 + 16 = 19 = N − 1.
df(A × B) = df(A) × df(B) df Within = N − number of cells
🪝 Memory hook
Everything is “minus one”, except the interaction, which multiplies.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“Each factor loses one degree of freedom for its own levels, the interaction gets the two leftovers multiplied together, and whatever’s left over belongs to the people.”
★ Key insight
In a 2 × 2 all three effect df equal 1, which makes it a flattering example. Do one calculation for a 2 × 3 before the exam, where df(A × B) = 1 × 2 = 2, so the rule is not hiding behind ones.

Lecture 11 of 16 · the table · slides 33

📋 The finished ANOVA table — three F values

One MS Within, three F values

Now everything goes into one table. Each effect gets its own mean square by dividing along its own row, and then each F is that mean square divided by the SAME MS Within. One noise estimate; three signals.

  • ➗MS = SS ÷ df, computed along the row, exactly as in a one-factor ANOVA.
  • 🎯MS Within = 1280 ⁄ 16 = 80. This is the denominator of all three F values.
  • 💚Fan: MS = 320, F = 320 ⁄ 80 = 4.00, reported as F(1, 16) = 4.00.
  • 🎧Sound: MS = 720, F = 720 ⁄ 80 = 9.00. Interaction: MS = 500, F = 500 ⁄ 80 = 6.25.
  • ✍️Every F is written with its own numerator df first and the shared df Within second.
F(effect) = MS(effect) ÷ MS Within MS Within = SS Within ÷ df Within
🪝 Memory hook

★ Key insight

Lecture 13 of 16 · the key idea · slides 22, 35

🔀 What an interaction actually is

The effect of one thing depends on the other

The lecture’s definition: two variables interact if a particular COMBINATION of them leads to results that would not have been anticipated from the main effects alone. Equivalently — and this is the version to memorise — an interaction means the effect of one variable is different at different levels of the other.

  • 2️⃣For non-fans, Michael beats the weather report by 22 − 20 = 2 points.
  • 2️⃣2️⃣For fans, Michael beats the weather report by 40 − 18 = 22 points.
  • 🔀Two different effects of the same manipulation. That difference IS the interaction.
  • 🔁Read the other way round: within the weather report, fans score 18 − 20 = −2; within Michael Jackson they score 40 − 22 = +18.
  • 🗣️Both readings describe the same interaction. The lecture says to use whichever one makes more sense.
interaction present ⇔ (effect of A at B₁) ≠ (effect of A at B₂)
🪝 Memory hook
“It depends.” If the honest answer to “does A work?” is “it depends on B”, that is an interaction.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“Michael Jackson barely helps if you’re not a fan — two points. If you ARE a fan it adds twenty-two. So whether the music works depends entirely on who’s listening.”
★ Key insight
An interaction is never “A and B both matter”. That is two main effects. An interaction is specifically that the SIZE or DIRECTION of one factor’s effect changes across the levels of the other.
⚠ A note on these numbers
A real-world case of the same shape, from the lecture: smoking raises the risk of throat cancer, alcohol raises it, and doing both raises it far more than adding the two separate risks would suggest.

📊 Reading the picture — parallel or not

Parallel means no interaction. That is the whole rule.

The lecture plots the cell means and says it plainly: parallel lines mean NO interaction, non-parallel lines mean there IS one. It then lays out the combinations you can meet — neither factor significant, one factor, both factors, each with or without an interaction.

  • 📈Put one factor on the x-axis, draw one line per level of the other, and mark the cell means.
  • 📏PARALLEL lines → no interaction. The gap between the lines is the same everywhere.
  • ✖️NON-PARALLEL (including crossing) → interaction. Crossing is just an extreme case.
  • ↕️A main effect of the x-axis factor shows as lines that slope IN THE SAME OVERALL DIRECTION; a main effect of the other factor shows as lines separated vertically.
  • ⚖️The picture suggests; only the F for the interaction decides whether the non-parallelism is more than noise.
🪝 Memory hook
Parallel = no interaction. Not parallel = interaction. Nothing else to see.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“I just draw the four averages as two lines. If the lines run parallel, the two things don’t depend on each other. If they fan apart or cross, they do.”
★ Key insight
A plot can only ever show you a sample. Two lines are almost never exactly parallel in real data, which is precisely why the interaction gets its own F test rather than being judged by eye.

Lecture 15 of 16 · why and when · slides 41–42

✅ Advantages — and the three cautions

Why two factors beat two separate studies

The lecture closes the methodology with why you would run a two-factor design at all, and what to be careful about once you have the output. The last advantage is the decisive one: an interaction cannot be tested any other way.

  • ⚡MORE EFFICIENT: two factors investigated in one study instead of two.
  • 👥SMALLER SAMPLES PER CONDITION: everyone at every level of B also contributes to the comparison for A.
  • 🔇LESS RESIDUAL VARIATION: in a one-way ANOVA on A, any effect of B goes into the error term and inflates it.
  • 🔀INTERACTIONS BECOME TESTABLE — and they cannot be tested with a series of one-way ANOVAs.
  • ⚠️The cautions: always plot the factor-level means; examine the interaction FIRST; and where there is an interaction, examine the CELL means, because the marginal means no longer tell the whole story.
🪝 Memory hook
Interaction first, then the main effects. And always draw the picture.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“Running the two factors together is cheaper, cleaner and the only way to ask whether they depend on each other. And when they do, I have to describe the four groups, not the two averages.”
★ Key insight
“Examine the interaction first” is a rule about interpretation, not arithmetic. If the interaction is significant, a sentence like “Michael Jackson raises scores” is true on average and misleading in practice — it barely raises them for non-fans.
🎬 Watch: Advantages🎬 Watch: a two-factor worked example
🧩 Comprehension check
Why does a two-factor design reduce residual variation compared with a one-way ANOVA on the same data?
Because the variation caused by the second factor is pulled OUT of the error term and given its own row. A smaller MS Within means a larger F for the factor you cared about.
Could you test the interaction by running a one-way ANOVA on Sound for the fans and another one for the non-fans?
No. That gives you two separate effects but no test of whether they DIFFER from each other. The lecture states this directly: interactions cannot be tested with a series of one-way ANOVAs.
Lecture 16 of 16 · literature · slides 43–48

📚 Dove, Byrne & Bruce (2009) — the same logic in a real study

A 2 × 3 ANOVA from Behaviour Research and Therapy

The lecture ends with a published 2 × 3 ANOVA. Seventy-six overweight and obese women in a twelve-week cognitive behavioural therapy programme were measured for depression with the Beck Depression Inventory-II. The two factors were BMI category (overweight, obese, severely obese) and dichotomous — all-or-nothing — thinking, scored high or low.

  • 📊Main effect of BMI category: F(2, 70) = 8.28, p < 0.01 — significant.
  • ❌Main effect of dichotomous thinking: F(1, 70) = 0.92, p = 0.34 — not significant.
  • ✅BMI × dichotomous thinking interaction: F(2, 70) = 5.53, p < 0.01 — significant.
  • 🔗Unpacking it: among women LOW in dichotomous thinking, depression and BMI had a partial correlation of r = 0.48, p < 0.01.
  • 0️⃣Among women HIGH in dichotomous thinking that partial correlation vanished: r = 0.00, p = 0.99.
🪝 Memory hook
Same shape as Michael Jackson: no main effect for the second factor, but it decides everything.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“Heavier women were more depressed — but only if they weren’t all-or-nothing thinkers. For the all-or-nothing thinkers there was no relationship at all. So the thinking style changes whether BMI matters.”
★ Key insight
Dichotomous thinking shows NO main effect — on its own it does not raise or lower depression — and yet it completely controls whether BMI relates to depression. Report only the main effects here and you would miss the entire finding.

🏛️ Synthesis — the whole lecture in one paragraph

A two-factor ANOVA takes the machine you already know and splits its between-groups part into three. Two factors sort people into cells; the spread of those cell means around the grand mean is SS Between, and it divides into SS(A), SS(B) and whatever is left over, which is the interaction. The degrees of freedom divide the same way, with the interaction df a product rather than a sum. Each piece becomes a mean square and is divided by one shared MS Within, giving three F values tested against the same table. The two main effects ask whether each factor matters on its own, averaging the other away; the interaction asks whether the effect of one depends on the level of the other, which is the one question a stack of separate one-way ANOVAs can never answer. And it is the question that usually matters most: in the lecture’s own example fandom has no main effect at all, and yet it decides whether the music adds two points or twenty-two — exactly as, in Dove, Byrne and Bruce (2009), a thinking style with no main effect on depression decides whether depression tracks BMI at all. Which is why the rule is: plot the cell means, read the interaction first, and only then tell a story about the main effects.

🏷️ The interaction is the only question the design uniquely unlocks. That is why it exists.
Question 17 · lecture 16
In Dove, Byrne &amp; Bruce (2009), dichotomous thinking had no main effect, F(1, 70) = 0.92, p = 0.34, but the BMI × thinking interaction was significant. What does that mean?
  • a.the study was underpowered, so the whole set of results should be ignored
  • b.dichotomous thinking lowers depression, though only by a small amount
  • c.thinking style does not shift depression itself, but decides whether BMI tracks it
  • d.BMI and dichotomous thinking are strongly correlated with one another
No main effect means thinking style does not shift depression by itself. The interaction means it changes what BMI does: the depression–BMI correlation was 0.48 among low dichotomous thinkers and 0.00 among high ones.
The other options
✗a. A significant interaction was detected, so the design was not too weak to find the effect it was looking for.
✗b. No main effect means no overall shift in either direction.
✗d. An interaction is about the effect on the outcome, not a correlation between the two factors.
🏷️ The same shape as the lecture’s own example: no main effect, and yet it decides everything.
Question 18 · lecture 13
In the lecture’s data, non-fans score 20 (weather) and 22 (Michael); fans score 18 (weather) and 40 (Michael). Which statement reports the interaction correctly?
  • a.Michael Jackson raised the score by about 12 points on average
  • b.Michael Jackson added 2 points for non-fans and 22 points for fans
  • c.fans scored higher than non-fans in both conditions
  • d.the weather report lowered scores for everybody
An interaction is reported as two different simple effects: 22 − 20 = 2 among non-fans and 40 − 18 = 22 among fans. The same manipulation did two very different things.
The other options
✗a. 12 is the main effect (31 − 19). True on average, and it hides exactly the thing the question is asking about.
✗c. Not true — in the weather condition the fans scored LOWER, 18 against 20.
✗d. There is no before-and-after here, only a comparison between groups.
🏷️ Report an interaction with two numbers, one per level of the other factor. One average number is a main effect.

Question 20 · lecture 15
According to the lecture’s cautions, in what order should you look at the output?
  • a.plot the means, read the interaction, then interpret the main effects
  • b.read the main effects first, and only check the interaction if one is significant
  • c.read the main effects first, because they have the larger sums of squares

The lecture’s cautions are: always plot the factor-level means; examine the interaction FIRST; and when there is an interaction, examine the cell means, because the marginal means do not tell the whole story.
The other options
✗b. The interaction is checked first precisely because it changes how the main effects should be read.
